Prologue

Alden: I wonder… if Oppenheimer had one more shot, just one rewind… would he still light the match?

Free preview15 Seconds to Ruin · D’Artagnan

“Alden Kray,” I say into the mic, my voice cracking like a preteen in a school recital. Great. Real smooth.

“Voice scan recognized,” the computer responds in its usual overly calm, judgmental monotone.

Okay. Okay. Deep breath. In through the nose, out through the—nope, nope, too fast. Try again. Okay.

I wipe my palms on my jeans. Twice. They’re still clammy. Possibly wetter now, actually. Physics is broken.

Okay. Door’s opening. It’s fine. Totally fine.

I’m in. I’m in.

Six floors underground. No windows. No noise. Just me, oh and rows upon rows of servers and computers with flickering green and amber lights, and a small black box that hums like it knows secrets. Which, I mean… it does.

The vault is… how do I put this? It’s like the Pentagon and a Bond villain’s lair had a baby and then raised it inside a Faraday cage. The walls are layered with ballistic polymer, Kevlar mesh, lead shielding, and some kind of proprietary graphene composite that costs more than my entire existence. "It smells like a fusion of cold steel and static—part hospital, part data center." There’s this faint hum in the air, a vibration in your teeth. I keep licking my lips. I don’t know why. Maybe because it tastes like static in here. Like electricity is trying to get inside you.

You don’t get into this vault with a key or a badge. Oh no. This place is insane. You start by standing on a platform that scans your skeletal structure. Bones. Not fingerprints—those are child's play. Then there's the blood sample via nanoneedle, the voice phrase which, today, is: “Cicadas don’t lie in winter,”—what does that even mean? Anyway—and then, yeah, a scream. A literal scream. They use it to map your panic resonance. Mine always cracks on the last octave.

Then comes the final test. The big one. The one that makes my armpits break into cold-sweat karaoke.

Micro-expression scan.

Voice stress analysis.

Pupil dilation. Skin conductivity. Capillary dilation.

Basically, it’s a lie detector from the future… designed by people who don’t blink. Ever.

And me?

I blink too fast. Every. Single. Time.

It’s like my eyelids are training for a speed-skating event.

I swear I’m going to start practicing at home—just… sitting in front of the mirror like some blinking weirdo. Blink. Pause. Blink. Normal human blinks. Nothing suspicious here, officer.

One of these days, this thing’s going to flag me for high treason just because my eyeballs can’t chill.

Sigh, I digress.

Only three people have access to the vault:

Me. Because, well… I built parts of what it stores. Okay, half of it. Maybe more.

Eustachy H. Brand, because he owns it.

Maya Chen. The security overlord who eats world-class hackers for breakfast and doesn't change facial expressions unless there’s actual gunfire.

Outside? Oh boy. Yeah - armed guards. Like, really armed. Not private security. Not ex-Marines. These guys are ghosts. Cold, silent, wired for violence. They don’t talk. They don’t smile, they don’t exist in any database I’ve ever found—and trust me, I’ve looked. They wear black body armor, helmets with mirrored visors, and carry rifles that hum like coiled cobras. No names. Just call signs. One’s leaning against the wall with a toothpick. The other five don’t move. At all. I’m not even sure they breathe.

I once accidentally tripped the secondary motion grid—just testing a theory, totally academic—and within four seconds they had me surrounded. No shouting. No questions. Just...guns pointed at my head. I didn’t sleep for a week. I still have night sweats thinking about it. They’re not here for my safety. They’re here in case I ever become a problem.

Inside? It’s… silent. Not regular quiet—this is surgical silence. Refrigerated silence. The kind that seeps into your bones and makes your teeth feel cold. Like walking into a morgue at midnight during a power outage. My breath fogs just a little, and that’s saying something considering the air’s being triple-filtered and recycled faster than my thoughts right now.

The air smells sterile—metal and ozone, with the faint sting of some industrial solvent clinging to the ducts. It tastes like static, like putting your tongue on a battery. Which I did once, in middle school. That’s how I know.

And there it is. The reason for all the drama. The black box.

Nestled on a raised pedestal like some unholy idol, behind three layers of glass so thick it could stop a meteor. Bulletproof. Blast resistant, surrounded by nitrogen coolant pipes and fiber lines thicker than my leg. Its screen pulses—green, amber, green—like it’s breathing. Mocking me.

I hate that box.

I sit on the bench in front of it, elbows on knees, tie clenched between my teeth like a bit. I don’t even realize I’m grinding my molars until my jaw pulses with heat. I close my eyes for half a second and all I can think about is Oppenheimer.

Oppenheimer. Yeah. That guy. The “I am become death” guy. Built the atomic bomb, then immediately regretted it when—oh right—it turned cities into ash and people into shadows on concrete.

I think about him a lot lately. About how those scientists must’ve felt in that first split second when the bomb actually worked. The shock. The thrill. The horrible, spine-tingling pride of having done it.

And then came Hiroshima.

Now… here I sit. In my own desert. My own Trinity test.

And my version of the atomic bomb?

I succeeded.

The Black Box works. Oh, it works too well.

Eustachy H. Brand was clever—paranoid, maybe, but clever. He had me build the core architecture, compartmentalized the rest. Two other engineers worked on isolated subsystems, like puzzle pieces they didn’t know fit together. Then he had me connect them. And by the time I figured out what it all was… it was already too late.

You build something to protect your people. To beat the bad guys. To outsmart competitors. Maybe to be remembered.

Then you watch it twist. Mutate. Burn the sky instead.

That’s what this is. This thing doesn’t vaporize cities. It vaporizes truth. Identities. Reputations. It rewrites public perception—boosts some stories, buries others. Spins entire realities. No need for bullets. Just… narrative.

And yeah. I built it.

I want to shut it down. I really do. Every day. But if I do that… Brand finds out. Then Maya. And then… I disappear. No obituary. No search. Just static. And my family? My wife, my daughters, my mom, brother? Gone too. Quietly. Without headlines. Without trace.

Because this isn’t just a media company. It’s a black hole with a press badge. It’s a $2 trillion black hole. Digital media alone pulls in over a trillion dollars. TV and streaming, another $800 billion. Social media? Sixty billion in ad spend last year. That’s sixty billion dollars to convince you what to think. I did the math once—don’t ask how long it took—that’s almost $40 million for every single dollar the average household makes.

You drop two million dollars on the ground if you're Brand? It’s like someone with a $70k salary losing seventy cents. You don’t even look back. Brand wouldn’t even bend over to pick it up.

Right. Sorry. Tangent.

Where was I?

Oh. Yeah. The Black Box.

People think it’s some metaphor. No. It’s an actual black box. Matte finish. No logos. No ports. No buttons. Just pulsing with that sickly green light—like a heart monitor for the cyber soul. Inside? A cluster of quantum processors spinning faster than my anxiety at 3 a.m.

And when I say "quantum," I mean actual entanglement-based decision trees. It doesn’t just crunch data. It feels through it. It intuits your digital life. Your beliefs. Your intentions.

I wrote part of the inference engine. Not all of it. Just the probabilistic classifier that predicts ideological alignment based on digital breadcrumbs. Your posts, your likes, your buying habits, your pause time on videos. Even what kinds of thumbnails you scroll past without clicking. It's all a signature. A personality fingerprint.

That fingerprint gets dropped into one of five categories.

“Category 1,” I whisper, tying and untying a loose thread on my sleeve, “your worldview aligns with the media’s agenda and—and this part’s important—you’re influential. Like, real influential. We’re talking actors, athletes, high-tier politicians, billionaires with yachts named after planets.

These people? When they mess up—and they always do—the black box buries the stories. Covers it up. Not erased, that’d be obvious. Just… softened. Edited. The machine finds your skeletons and politely shuts the closet door. It protects the brand.”

“Category 2,” I mutter, scratching the back of my neck. “You align, but you’re not influential. You’re like… a dentist from Peoria. You’ve got a TikTok with 400 followers and a bunch of posts about your golden retriever. You’ll get boosted occasionally, maybe used in a campaign as a ‘real person.’ But mostly? You’re background noise. Safe. For now.”

“Category 3,” I whisper, curling my toes inside my sneakers. “You’re harmless. Nobody reads your blog. You’ve got twelve Facebook friends and half of them are bots. You rant about local taxes and still own a fax machine. You’re safe.”

“Category 4.”

“You’re influential, but unpredictable. You post inspirational stuff but then slip in one off-brand comment about media consolidation or foreign policy or pharmaceutical lobbying and—boom—you’re dangerous.”

Not enough to cancel you. That would make you a martyr. No, no—they monitor you. Feed the algorithm with shadow comments. Deboost your reach. Quietly signal to brand sponsors that you might not be “safe.” Your comments get filled with bots. Trolls. Misinformation. And eventually… you start to question yourself. Or burn out.

“Category 5.”

I swallow hard. My chest gets tight, my tie ends up in my mouth again.

“You’re misaligned,” I say, almost reverently. “And influential.”

You're a celebrity with your own mind. A billionaire who funds independent journalism. A viral creator who tells uncomfortable truths. You challenge narratives. You don’t follow ‘their’ scripts. You ask questions that aren’t supposed to be asked.

You are public enemy #1.

They mark you.

And if you stumble? They report it—they weaponize it. They loop it on every platform, spin it like a top until it drills into the earth. They pump it full of fake information. And if your record’s clean and they can’t find dirt, no problem— they invent some, they’ll smear mud until you look like a swamp creature.

They own the distribution. The narrative. The outrage machine.

You won’t just get buried—you’ll vanish beneath an avalanche of pixels and push notifications, opinion pieces and hit reels, all timestamped and algorithm-tuned to outlive your side of the story.

And the scariest part?

No one will even remember what was real to begin with.

And the Black Box? It’s not just a monitor. It’s the choreographer. It nudges headlines into certain time slots. Boosts posts. It doesn’t just tell stories—it rewrites your character arc.

I lean back in my chair, fingers twitching at the edge of my keyboard, eyes locked on the thing behind the glass.

Still humming. Still blinking. Still deciding who gets to be the hero this week—and who disappears under a scandal they never saw coming.

I wonder… if Oppenheimer had one more shot, just one rewind… would he still light the match?
